Priced In, Bit by Bit: Measuring Information Aggregation in Prediction Markets

September 2026

PDF Code

Prediction markets are typically evaluated on whether their prices are well calibrated forecasts of eventual outcomes. This paper asks a different question: how informative are prices? Using Shannon entropy and mutual information on a panel of roughly 50,000 closed binary markets on Polymarket, we find that observing prices on average resolves 38.3% of uncertainty surrounding outcomes, and less in markets that are part of a set of mutually exclusive outcomes. Among markets open for over four months, which are more informative than average, prices reduce 42.2% of uncertainty four months from resolution, rising to 85.4% one day from resolution. Finally, following Samuelson’s martingales, we show that while price changes are statistically predictable, any departure from a fair game is economically negligible. These findings are largely consistent with Hayek’s claim that prices aggregate dispersed information.

Keywords: prediction markets, information aggregation, market efficiency, mutual information, entropy

Drawing the Last Straw: Competition and Deception in a Duopoly

Professor Nagpaul Research Fellowship, advised by Prof. Anant Singh. May 2026

PDF

Deception, such as hidden fees or misleading product names, makes an offer look better, but only up to a point. The deception that pushes a consumer past it is the last straw, and she abandons the firm. How much it takes depends on what she knows, and firms rarely know how much their customers will notice. We study a duopoly in which consumers buy from the most deceptive firm whose deception they have not detected. When firms know how discerning consumers are, competition escalates deception, and in the mixed-strategy equilibrium average deception exceeds the monopoly level by about 41 per cent. When they do not, uncertainty cuts both ways. If an undiscerning market is unlikely, firms deceive less on average, and earn less, than if they knew. If it is likely enough, firms gamble on it, and consumers periodically see through a firm and abandon it outright. These collapses are an equilibrium outcome, and they are most likely when firms are fairly, but not fully, confident that their customers will not notice.

Keywords: deception, consumer tolerance, incomplete information, oligopoly, behavioral industrial organization

Income Segregation in an Agent-Based Housing Market

Code

Schelling showed that mild preferences over one’s neighbors can produce stark segregation. This project asks what happens when those preferences have to be paid for. Agents with incomes drawn from a Delhi income distribution estimated from CMIE data rent homes across 100 neighborhoods. Each agent values two things, combined in a Cobb–Douglas utility function: the share of their neighbors who earn at least as much as they do, and the share of their housing budget left after rent. As in Schelling, only agents who are unhappy with their neighborhood search for a new one. Each searching agent bids its bid-rent, the highest rent at which a home is still better than nonmarket housing, and the same rule decides when a tenant is priced out. Rents, moves and displacement therefore all follow from a single set of preferences. Sorting is measured by decomposing the Theil index into inequality within and between neighborhoods.

The model is then used to evaluate an affordable-housing set-aside that reserves 20% of each neighborhood’s homes at 60% of the market rent for the poorest 30% of earners. Welfare is measured as each agent’s equivalent variation as a share of income, comparing every agent with themselves in a matched baseline city. The set-aside lowers welfare for the median agent by about 3.5% of income, and two-thirds of agents are worse off, including most of those it targets. Because the discount is pegged to market rents, reserved homes in expensive neighborhoods stay unaffordable to the households eligible for them. They sit empty, market supply shrinks, and rents rise across the income distribution: the poorest tenth’s rent share goes from 14% to 20% of income, and the share of agents in nonmarket housing rises from 6.8% to 12.1%.

Keywords: residential segregation, Schelling model, agent-based modeling, housing markets, inclusionary zoning

Profits, Prices, and the Power of Information

with Madhura Mukund and Tanishi Singhal. Vitta, Shri Ram College of Commerce, 2025

Selected as one of 10 articles from over 1,700 submissions across five countries.

PDF

The price mechanism aggregates dispersed information only when someone trades on it, and we argue that profit, as the opportunity cost of keeping private information secret, is what draws that information into prices. Following Grossman and Stiglitz, some inefficiency must persist for this incentive to survive, which sets off a race to find and exploit it. Renaissance Technologies’ Medallion Fund shows the race at its best, with quantitative models improving price discovery; SEBI’s allegations against Jane Street show its dark reflection, where profit comes from injecting noise into prices rather than information.

State Data Centre Policy Index

with Nathanya Eby Thomas. August 2026

PDF

The project aims to build a common, points-based framework to compare the Data Centre policies notified by Indian states, and to test how robust the resulting comparison is. The study covers 17 states with a publicly available Data Centre policy, or with explicit Data Centre provisions inside a broader industrial or IT policy. Each state was scored against a 33-incentive Scorecard, with a maximum of 132 points, grouped into five policy dimensions. A 20,000-trial Monte Carlo simulation was then used to test how sensitive the resulting state ranking is to the weight given to each of the five dimensions.

The Monte Carlo results show that states competing mainly on the size of their fiscal package face the greatest rank instability. Rajasthan’s balanced profile across all five dimensions is what makes it the only state holding a Top 4 position in all 20,000 trials. States should treat dimensional balance, not incentive size alone, as the design objective.
