About

I study economics at St. Stephen's College, University of Delhi, and expect to graduate in 2027. In 2025 I was the Professor Nagpaul Fellow at the college's Department of Mathematics, where I worked on game theory with incomplete information.

My research is about information: how much of it market prices actually aggregate, and how strategic agents behave when they cannot identify the state of the world they are in. The work tends to be computational as well as theoretical — a 43-million-row panel of prediction market prices, a million-agent housing market, a two-period Bayesian duopoly.

Interests: information economics, market efficiency and price discovery, game theory with incomplete information, computational and agent-based methods.

Priced In, Bit by Bit: Measuring Information Aggregation in Prediction Markets

September 2026

How much of an event's outcome uncertainty is resolved by its market price, measured in bits, across 50,000 Polymarket markets.

Paper Code and data

Abstract

Prediction markets are typically evaluated on whether their prices are well calibrated forecasts of eventual outcomes. This paper asks a different question: how informative are prices? We use Shannon entropy and mutual information to quantify how much information observing prices tells us about the final outcome. Using a panel of roughly 50,000 closed binary markets on Polymarket, we find that observing prices on average resolves 53.65% of uncertainty surrounding outcomes, consistent across different market structures. Even over four months from resolution, prices reduce 36.36% of uncertainty, that figure jumping up to 90% one day from resolution. Finally, following Samuelson's martingales, we show that while price changes are statistically predictable, any departure from a fair game is economically negligible, consistent with prices closely following an efficient information aggregation process. These findings are largely consistent with Hayek's claim that prices aggregate dispersed information, with only small, though significant, departures from perfect efficiency.

Drawing the Last Straw: Competition and Deception in a Duopoly

Professor Nagpaul Fellowship, advised by Prof. Anant Singh. May 2026.

What happens to deceptive marketing when firms cannot learn the demand regime they are facing from the sales they observe.

Paper

Abstract

I develop a two-period Bayesian duopoly in which firms choose how much to deceive consumers whose tolerance for deception is unknown, and in which observed sales cannot identify true market demand because demand mappings overlap across regimes. Heterogeneity in beliefs generates a unique asymmetric Bayesian Nash equilibrium with strategic complementarity in deceptive marketing. I show that competition amplifies belief errors rather than correcting them, creating a strictly positive probability of complete market collapse.

Income Segregation in an Agent-Based Housing Market

Since August 2025

Extends Schelling's segregation model with a budget-constrained rental market. Agents hold Cobb–Douglas preferences over neighborhood income composition and residual consumption, calibrated to Delhi income microdata from the CMIE. Sealed-bid auctions across 100 neighborhoods per round set rents endogenously and displace priced-out tenants; sorting is tracked by Theil decomposition into within- and between-neighborhood inequality over a population of one million agents. A below-market-rate set-aside is simulated against a Monte Carlo baseline to measure targeting by income bracket.

State Data Center Policy Competitiveness Index

with Nathanya Eby Thomas. August 2026.

A 17-state policy index constructed from state government policy documents. I contributed the empirical analysis: a Monte Carlo sensitivity study over 20,000 Dirichlet-distributed dimension-weight vectors, using state-level rank distributions and dimension-sensitivity diagnostics to quantify rank stability and identify the policy dimensions that drive rank changes.
